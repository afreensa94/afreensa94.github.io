Nuances of Forced Migration

An interactive e-learning course on the systems behind forced displacement, evaluated with a pre/post-test design measuring both knowledge gain and empathy shift.

Role

Lead Instructional Designer & UX Researcher

Format

6 modules, 15–30 min

Methods

Pre/post-test, Likert, open-ended reflection

The Problem

Most educational content on forced migration stays at the surface — conflict as a headline cause, displacement as a single event rather than a system. The goal here was to build a course that could hold the layered complexity of forced migration (conflict, persecution, climate disaster, economic hardship, and the policies that trap people in motion) without losing high school and college-aged learners, and to do it in a way that represented lived experience with dignity rather than as an abstraction.

Design goals going in: simplify complexity without compromising depth, apply ELSI (Ethical, Legal, Social Implications) principles to the topic, build a framework for layered learning that moves from personal impact to global systems, and evoke empathy without exploiting the subject matter.

Methodology

Learning outcomes were tested using a pre/post-test design with 6 participants — graduate students with varying prior knowledge of forced migration, recruited by convenience sampling. The sample was intentionally kept small to respect time constraints, with the known trade-off of limited statistical generalizability.

Instruments

  • Pre-test (8 questions, multiple choice) — mapped to LO1: foundational knowledge of forced migration's causes and affected populations.
  • Post-test (10 questions — multiple choice, Likert confidence rating, and an open-ended reflection) — mapped across LO1, LO2, and LO3.

The Likert and open-ended questions weren't in the original design — they were added mid-process, after early testing showed the multiple-choice-only instrument couldn't capture whether the course was actually shifting empathy, only whether it was transferring facts. That revision is the methodological decision I'd point to first: recognizing an instrument doesn't measure what you actually care about, and fixing it before running the full study rather than after.

Findings

Knowledge (LO1)

Most participants entered with pre-existing foundational knowledge, so post-test responses showed nuanced shifts rather than dramatic gains — one participant moved from selecting "All of the above" to identifying a specific cause (persecution and political instability), suggesting the course deepened understanding rather than introducing it from zero.

Emotional Understanding (LO2)

A branching, choice-based module — built with the same interactive-narrative mechanics used in narrative games — placed learners directly in the role of a displaced individual facing survival decisions. It was the primary driver of LO2 outcomes: post-test responses shifted from factual recall toward personal, emotional reflection specifically after that module.

Empathy & Behavioral Insight (LO3)

2 of 6 participants explicitly expressed empathy in their open-ended post-test responses, with several expressing a desire to help create safer spaces for displaced people — a behavioral-intention shift that went beyond what the course's stated knowledge objectives were even asking for.

Design implications

  • Scenario-based learning drove the affective outcomes — the branching, choice-based module outperformed the purely informational modules on every empathy-related measure, which reprioritized where design effort went in later revisions.
  • Cognitive and emotional load had to be balanced deliberately — keeping the course to 15–30 minutes across segmented modules was a direct response to the subject's emotional weight, not just a pacing preference.
  • Pre-existing knowledge reframed the goal — with an already-informed audience, the design objective shifted from teaching facts to deepening perspective, which changed how later modules were written.

Constraints

Small sample size (n=6) limits statistical significance and generalizability. Convenience sampling from one population may not reflect how more diverse learners would respond. Self-reported empathy is also difficult to validate on its own terms — these responses reflect stated intention, not observed behavior, and I'd want a behavioral or longitudinal follow-up before treating the empathy findings as more than directional.
