Infinity Castle

A game prototype with no mouse or keyboard — a hand-tracked katana is the only interface. Currently in research toward simulating the sword's physical weight through embodied interaction.

Solo designer & developer

Stack

Where the idea came from

The starting point was a specific, personal one: Demon Slayer's Infinity Castle arc — an ever-shifting fortress where the story's swordfights take place — and wanting to actually feel what it would be like to be inside it, katana in hand, rather than just watch it. That's a feeling, not a mechanic, and figuring out how to make it a mechanic is where the research came in.

Around the same time, I was reflecting on a paper about VR/AR fidelity — visual, aural, behavioral, psychological, and haptic realism — in the context of a different narrative game project. One finding stood out more than the rest: the study's participants didn't just want visual realism, they wanted physical engagement — the sensation of weight in an object that had none.

That surfaced an old memory: a childhood game in which a friend would mime touching my hands while my eyes were closed, and on opening them, I'd find my arms genuinely heavy to lift. Nothing had physically changed, but my brain's interpretation of the input had. It's a real perceptual illusion, and it pointed to an actual design question: if the brain can be convinced of a weight that isn't there, could a game simulate the heft of a weapon through embodied cues alone, with no physical resistance behind it?

That question didn't fit the project it came from, but it was exactly the mechanism Infinity Castle needed — a sword-focused, embodied space is precisely where a weight illusion matters most. So it became its own prototype instead.

The interaction model

Infinity Castle removes the mouse and keyboard entirely. The katana's tip — tracked through the player's hand via MediaPipe — is the single interaction point the whole game is built around. Aiming and navigating aren't separate actions; pointing where you want to go is the input.

Grip

Close the hand into a fist to grip the katana — the player advances automatically while gripped.

Steer

Point the tip left or right of vertical to turn in that direction.

Slash

A swinging motion triggers an attack.

Stop

Opening the palm releases the grip and halts movement.

Collapsing navigation and combat into one gesture-driven object was a deliberate constraint — the goal isn't just "hands-free controls," it's making the katana feel like an extension of the player's own hand rather than a separate tool being operated.

Current work — simulating weight

The literature review that started this project also surfaced a useful framing: of the fidelity types (visual, aural, behavioral, psychological, haptic), the paper argued behavioral and emotional fidelity matter most for immersion — and that haptic fidelity, while harder to fake, can be approximated through indirect cues rather than real physical feedback. That's the specific research question I'm working through now: what visual, motion, and timing cues can substitute for the physical resistance a real katana would have, so the weapon reads as heavy even though the player is holding nothing?

This is still open. The interaction model works — grip, steer, slash, stop are all functional in V1 — but the weight-simulation layer is research in progress, not yet implemented. The honest state of this project is: working gesture-controlled prototype, unsolved embodied-weight problem.

Constraints

This is a solo build, currently running locally and not yet publicly hosted. MediaPipe's hand-tracking accuracy under real-world lighting and camera conditions is also a real constraint on how precise the grip/point/swing gestures can be — part of what the current research phase has to account for, not just the perceptual-weight question on its own.
